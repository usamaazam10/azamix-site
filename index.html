{% include section-head.html no="01" label="Problems we help solve" id="problems-title" title="Where software earns its cost." lede="Custom software is one option, not the default. These are the situations where well-built software, or a well-chosen integration, usually helps. In each case we'll also check whether an existing product, or a change in process, would solve it more simply." %}

  • Manual, repetitive work

    The same data is typed into several places, reports are assembled by hand every week, and reminders depend on someone's memory. Predictable steps like these can usually be automated with ordinary code, freeing people for the work that needs judgement.

  • Systems that don't talk to each other

    Sales, accounts, stock and support each live in a different tool. Integrations can keep them in step automatically, so a change in one place appears everywhere it should, with a record of what was synced and when.

  • Outdated applications

    An older application still works, but every change is slow and risky, and it no longer fits how the business operates. Rewriting everything at once is rarely the answer. Modernising in stages, starting with the parts that cause the most friction, keeps the business running throughout.

  • A customer experience that needs a product

    Customers ask for things a website can't do: booking, ordering, tracking, managing their account. A mobile app or web portal can, provided its backend, support process and costs are planned along with it.

  • Internal workflows that leak time

    Approvals wait in someone's inbox, hand-offs get lost, and answering "how are we doing?" means collecting numbers from several people. A system built around the workflow can route the work, show what's stuck and produce reports from the source data.

  • Work that could be automated

    Some steps follow fixed rules and need only ordinary code. Others involve reading documents, sorting requests or drafting replies, where AI can sometimes help. We test a specific use case against real examples before anyone commits to building it, with human review where errors matter.

  1. Business operations and reporting systems

    One place for the records a business runs on, such as orders, jobs, customers or staff, with reports generated from that data instead of compiled by hand.

    Data model · roles · dashboards · exports

  2. Customer-facing mobile applications

    Apps for ordering, booking, tracking or managing an account, together with the backend and admin tools your team needs to run them.

    iOS · Android · API · notifications

  3. Internal management portals

    Browser-based tools for staff to approve requests, manage records and see what needs attention, with each person seeing only what their role allows.

    Authentication · permissions · audit trail

  4. Inventory and workflow tools

    Software that tracks items or tasks as they move through stages, so everyone knows what's in stock, what's waiting and what's stuck.

    Status flows · alerts · history

  5. API-connected platforms

    Products built around an API, so the web app, mobile app and partner systems all work from the same data and rules.

    REST API · documentation · versioning

  6. Automated reporting and integrations

    Scheduled jobs that collect data from the systems you already use, reconcile it and deliver reports or updates without anyone pressing a button.

    Scheduled jobs · webhooks · error alerts

{% include section-head.html no="05" label="Engineering principles" id="principles-title" title="What good software means to us, day to day." lede="Principles are easy to list. These are the decisions they lead to on a project, and why they matter once the software is in use." %}

  1. Maintainable architecture

    Code is organised so a new developer can find their way around it. Business rules live in one place rather than scattered across screens. Changing one business rule should mean changing one place in the code.

  2. Appropriate technology selection

    We pick established tools that suit the problem and that other developers can support, not the newest thing or whatever we used last time. Sometimes that means recommending an existing product instead of building one.

  3. Security and privacy

    Authentication, permissions, data handling and secrets management are planned at the start, not added at the end. We collect and keep only the data the software needs.

  4. Testing and reliability

    Important logic has automated tests, releases go through a repeatable pipeline, and errors are logged where someone will see them. Problems should surface before your users report them.

  5. Performance and accessibility

    Screens load quickly on ordinary devices and connections, and work with keyboards and screen readers. Accessibility is checked as we build, not audited afterwards.

  6. Documentation and knowledge transfer

    Setup instructions, architecture notes and the reasons behind major decisions are written down during the project, not reconstructed at the end. Handover includes walking your team, or your next developer, through the system. Knowledge shouldn't live in one person's head.

  7. Long-term maintainability

    Dependencies are kept to the ones that earn their place, upgrades are planned rather than postponed, and hosting, store and service accounts sit in your organisation's name. You should be able to run and extend the system without being locked in to us.

Azamix is a software development company. We design and build software for businesses: internal systems, mobile apps, web platforms, AI features and the integrations that connect them.

The problems we want to work on are practical ones: a workflow that takes too long, systems that don't share data, customers who need a better way to reach you. We'd rather recommend a simple, proven approach than an impressive one, and we'll say so when an existing product would serve you better than custom software.

We also take the unglamorous parts seriously: permissions, testing, documentation, backups and maintenance. They decide whether software is still useful in three years.
