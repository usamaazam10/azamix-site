*A note on interests: Azamix is a software development company, so we have a stake in this topic. The advice below is what we'd give a friend, and it applies to us as much as anyone.*

Choosing who builds your software is one of the decisions with the largest effect on whether a project succeeds. Polished websites and confident sales calls look much the same from the outside. The differences show up in how a company handles uncertainty, communication and ownership, and you can test for those before signing anything.

## Start with how they respond to your problem

The first conversation tells you a lot. Notice whether they:

- **Ask about the problem before the solution.** Who will use the software? What happens today? What does success look like? A company that jumps straight to features or technology is guessing.
- **Push back where it's warranted.** "Do you need an app for this, or would a web page do?" is a good sign. Agreeing with everything is not.
- **Say what they don't know.** Uncertainty about an integration or an AI feature, stated plainly, is more trustworthy than a confident promise.
- **Explain in plain language.** If you can't follow their explanation, you won't be able to follow the project.

## Be careful with early numbers

A fixed price or deadline offered before anyone understands the requirements is either padded heavily or likely to change. Neither is necessarily dishonest, but you should know which you're getting.

Better signs:

- An estimate tied to a written scope, with its assumptions listed.
- Clear identification of the riskiest parts.
- A suggestion to start with discovery or a smaller first version when the unknowns are large.

Ask: *"What would make this estimate wrong?"* A good answer is specific.

## Evaluating credibility

Portfolios are useful, but they're less straightforward than they look. A lot of business software is internal or confidential, so the most relevant work may not be shown publicly, and published screenshots don't tell you how maintainable the code is or how the project went.

Look for evidence you can actually check:

- **Can they explain technical decisions?** Ask why they'd choose a particular approach for your project, and what the alternatives are.
- **Do they write or talk publicly about their work?** Technical articles and documentation show how a team thinks.
- **References.** If they offer client references, speak to them, and ask about communication and problems, not just the result.
- **A small paid first phase.** A short discovery or prototype is the most reliable test of working together.

Be wary of claims you can't verify: large unexplained numbers, logos without context, or awards you've never heard of.

## Questions about how they build

| Ask | A good answer includes |
|---|---|
| How will we see progress? | Working software you can try at regular intervals, not only status reports |
| How do you test? | Automated tests for important logic, plus manual testing on real devices |
| How do you handle security? | Permissions enforced on the server, secrets kept out of code, dependencies kept updated |
| What happens when requirements change? | The effect on time and cost explained before work changes |
| What technology will you use, and why? | Established tools chosen for this project and for who'll maintain it |
| What documentation will we get? | How the system works, how to deploy it, and how to run it |

## Ownership and lock-in

These questions matter most in the long run, and they're the easiest to overlook:

- **Who owns the source code and other deliverables?** It should be written into the agreement.
- **Where does the code live?** Ideally in a repository your organisation controls or can access throughout.
- **Whose name are the accounts in?** Hosting, domains, app store developer accounts and third-party services should belong to your organisation.
- **Could another team take over?** Ask what would be needed. The answer should be "the repository, the documentation and access", not "us".

## After launch

Software needs maintenance: security updates, OS changes, dependency upgrades, fixes. Ask what support is available after launch, how it's priced, and what happens if you'd rather maintain it yourselves.

## Warning signs

- A complete fixed quote after one short call.
- Reluctance to put ownership terms in writing.
- No questions about your users or current process.
- Vague answers about testing, security or documentation.
- Pressure to decide quickly.
- Promises of results nobody could guarantee, such as "your app will be approved" or "the AI will be 99% accurate".

## A short checklist

1. Did they understand the problem, in your words?
2. Did they explain risks and alternatives?
3. Is the estimate tied to a written scope with assumptions?
4. Are ownership, accounts and handover clear?
5. Will you see working software regularly?
6. Could someone else maintain what they build?

If you can answer yes to all six, you're in a good position, whoever you choose.
